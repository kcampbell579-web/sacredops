Why your crew won't report near-misses (and how to fix it)

A near-miss is the cheapest safety lesson you will ever get — a warning with no injury, no damage, no cost. It is the exact event that, next time, becomes the real thing. And it is the one almost nobody reports.

Why crews do not report

It is tempting to call it a discipline problem — people do not care, train harder, remind more. But that is rarely it. Two things actually kill near-miss reporting:

  • Friction. Your best foreman will not stop a job with a truck at the dock and two guys short to go find a terminal, log in, and type. "Later" never comes.
  • Fear. If reporting a near-miss gets someone written up, reporting stops. Blame and data do not coexist.

How to fix it

  • Make it take 30 seconds. Let people report the way they already communicate — say it out loud or snap a photo, from the phone in their hand, at the moment it happens.
  • Separate reporting from blame. A near-miss is a gift, not a violation. Thank the person who raised it.
  • Act on them. Nothing kills reporting faster than reports that vanish. Close the loop and people keep reporting.

The bottom line

Every time reporting improves, it is not because people started caring more. It is because someone made it take less. Lower the friction and your leading indicators — the ones that prevent the real incident — finally show up.
